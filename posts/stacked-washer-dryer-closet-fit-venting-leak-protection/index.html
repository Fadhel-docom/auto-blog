Stackable Washer and Dryer in a Closet: Fit, Venting and Leak Protection for a Small Apartment

A stacked washer and dryer is the most space-efficient way to do laundry at home: two machines on the floor space of one. In a small apartment the natural home for the stack is a closet, like the one in the cover photo. A closet is also where a stack goes wrong, because a dryer behind closed doors needs air and an exhaust path, the door of the washer needs room to open, and the water connections are out of sight.

This guide takes the checks in order: measure, add clearances, size the door vents, plan the exhaust, check the floor and valves, then protect the closet from leaks and lint. The numbers differ by model, so it uses two manufacturers’ documents as worked examples. If you already have a laundry room and need shelves, see our small laundry room guide . If you have no closet at all, our guide to laundry without a laundry room covers drying and folding instead.

1. Start With the Manual, Not the Photo

Photos show a tidy stack behind a pair of doors. They do not show the numbers that decide whether it works: clearances, door open area, exhaust length and hose age. Those come from the manual of your own machines.

For this guide we read an LG installation manual for a compact 24-inch heat pump dryer and a GE spec sheet that lists the closet rules for its matching dryer. The figures are examples, not rules for every machine. Download the manual for your model and search it for “closet” and “stacked”.

2. Measure the Closet Three Ways

Measure width, depth and height at the narrowest point, not only at the doorway, because trim and brackets can take an inch. Measure the door opening too: the machines have to get through it.

Then add the clearances to the machine size. As a worked example, the LG manual gives a stacked compact pair that is 23 5/8 inches wide and 27 1/4 inches deep, with the top of the stack at 67 inches. It asks for 1 inch at each side and the front, 5 1/2 inches at the back and 6 inches below the ceiling. The closet must therefore be at least:

  • Width: 23 5/8 + 1 + 1 = 25 5/8 inches.
  • Depth: 27 1/4 + 1 (front) + 5 1/2 (back) = 33 3/4 inches.
  • Height: 67 + 6 = 73 inches.

Full-size machines are typically wider than this 24-inch example, so a closet that fits the compact pair may not fit yours.

3. Add the Clearances Your Model Asks For

Clearances are the gaps between the machines and the walls, door and ceiling, left for airflow, the exhaust duct and service. The documents disagree: LG asks for 1 inch at the sides and 5 1/2 inches behind a stacked pair, while the GE sheet lists 0 inches at the sides and 3 inches at the front and rear for its dryer. They describe different machines.

Use the larger of the manual’s figure and the space your duct needs, because a rigid elbow takes more depth than a bare wall. The LG manual also notes that a small gap stops vibration passing between appliances. Check that the washer door can open past the closet frame.

4. Size the Door Vents

A dryer behind closed doors needs air coming in and hot, humid air leaving, so both manuals require ventilated doors. LG asks for an upper opening of at least 48 square inches, no lower than 6 feet above the floor, and a lower opening of at least 24 square inches, no more than 1 foot above it; louvered doors with equivalent openings are accepted. The GE sheet asks for 60 square inches of open area for a dryer closet and 120 if the washer shares it.

Louvers are mostly slats, so count the gaps, not the panel: look for the free-area figure in the door’s specifications. Solid doors must be replaced or modified, which is a landlord decision (see section 11). The vent rules are written for closets with doors, so read your manual before running the dryer with them open.

5. Send the Dryer Exhaust Outdoors

Both documents say a dryer in a closet must exhaust to the outdoors. The US Fire Administration explains what building codes expect of the duct: metal with a smooth interior, firmly supported and sealed, no more than 35 feet long (shorter with turns), plus, for the short connector to the dryer, a listed flexible transition duct of no more than 8 feet that is not hidden in the construction. It discourages plastic and foil ducts, which can twist and trap lint.

In an apartment the duct usually runs through the wall behind the closet. If there is no exhaust opening, a vented dryer does not belong there. A heat pump dryer needs no outdoor duct, but the LG manual for the heat pump model we read still lists door vents for closets.

6. Check the Floor, Outlet and Water Valves

A stack is heavy and shakes when the drum spins. The LG manual asks for a solid floor with a slope of no more than 1 inch, able to carry the loaded weight of both machines, and a grounded outlet within 2 feet of either side of the dryer. Check the floor with a level.

You also need hot and cold shutoff valves and a drain connection you can still reach once the stack is in place. If they hide behind where the washer will sit, ask about moving them before the machines arrive.

7. Stack in the Right Order

Stack the dryer on top of the washer, with the manufacturer’s stacking kit. The GE sheet lists the brackets as a separate part, and the LG manual describes the dryer sitting on the washer. A stack needs a front-loading washer, because a top-loading washer cannot sit under a dryer. Do not improvise with straps or shims: a loose dryer on a spinning washer can walk.

Two people should lift the dryer, and the stack should be level before the first load. If the dryer is gas, the GE sheet adds that no other fuel-burning appliance may share the closet, and gas connections are a job for a licensed installer.

8. Protect the Closet From Leaks

The washer is the part of the stack that can damage a home fastest. State Farm advises checking washing machine hoses and replacing them every 3 to 5 years, sooner if you see cracking or bulging, and suggests considering a braided stainless steel hose with a built-in automatic shutoff. Behind closed doors a slow leak can go unnoticed for weeks, so add precautions of your own:

  • Put a shallow tray under the washer to catch drips, as in the cover photo. It will not hold a burst hose, so treat it as a warning aid.
  • Place a battery water sensor on the closet floor, where the first water collects.
  • Write the hose installation date on masking tape by the valve.
  • Close the supply valves when you leave for more than a day or two.

9. Keep Lint and Heat Under Control

The US Fire Administration counted about 2,900 clothes dryer fires in residential buildings a year from 2008 to 2010, with an average of 5 deaths, 100 injuries and $35 million in property loss. Failure to clean was the leading factor at 34 percent, and dust, fiber and lint were first ignited in 28 percent of fires. In a closet the lint has less room to escape, so the routine matters more:

  • Clean the lint filter and never run the dryer without it.
  • Have the vent duct cleaned periodically, and whenever drying takes longer than normal.
  • Check that the outdoor flap opens and that no nest blocks it.
  • Keep the area around the dryer free of items that can burn.
  • Unplug or disconnect the dryer if you will be away a long time.

10. Use the Space Above the Stack Carefully

The cover photo shows folded towels and a basket on a shelf just above the dryer. It looks tidy, but towels are exactly the kind of item that can burn. Your manual’s top clearance decides whether a shelf fits at all: the LG manual asks for 6 inches to the ceiling for a stacked pair, and 18 inches to an overhead shelf or cabinet for a single dryer.

Use the space outside the closet instead. A slim cart or wall shelf beside the doors can hold detergent and spare towels. Do not keep cleaning supplies in the closet; our guide to storing cleaning supplies safely shows where they fit better.

11. Renter Rules: Ask Before You Change Anything

Ask the landlord in writing before you buy: may you add or replace machines, is there an outdoor exhaust, are the valves and drain suitable, may the doors be changed for vents, and who pays for water damage? The LG manual also tells installers to check code requirements that limit or do not permit dryers in some places, such as sleeping quarters, and to contact the local building inspector.

12. Try This Weekend

  1. Measure the closet width, depth and height, and the door opening.
  2. Download the manual for your machines and note the clearances and vent figures.
  3. Add the clearances to the machine size and compare with your measurements.
  4. Estimate the open area of the door, or ask the maker.
  5. Check the washer hoses and write the date by the valve.
  6. Clean the lint filter and look at the outdoor vent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I put a stacked washer and dryer in a closet?

Yes, if the closet fits the machines plus the manual’s clearances, the doors are ventilated as required and the dryer exhausts outdoors or needs no exhaust. Check your lease and local codes first.

How big does the closet need to be?

Add the manual’s clearances to the machine size. One compact 24-inch LG stack needs an opening of about 25 5/8 inches wide, 33 3/4 deep and 73 high. Full-size machines need more, so use your own spec sheet.

Do closet doors need vents?

Yes. LG asks for 48 square inches of upper and 24 of lower opening, and GE asks for 60 square inches of open area, or 120 if the washer shares the closet. Louvers count only by their open area.

Which machine goes on top?

The dryer goes on the washer, with the manufacturer’s stacking kit. The stack needs a front-loading washer, because a top-loader cannot sit under a dryer. Never improvise the attachment with straps or shims.

How often should I replace the washer hoses?

State Farm advises checking them and replacing every 3 to 5 years, sooner if you see cracking or bulging. It also suggests a braided stainless steel hose with a built-in automatic shutoff.

How do I lower the risk of a dryer fire?

The US Fire Administration found failure to clean was the leading factor, at 34 percent. Clean the lint filter, have the vent cleaned periodically, use rigid metal duct and keep the area free of items that can burn.
