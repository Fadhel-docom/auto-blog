Most furniture that does not fit was measured once and in the wrong place. The room had a tape run along one wall, the sofa had a width on a product page, and nobody checked the doorway, the stairwell or the inch that a hinge and a doorknob take away. This guide uses a four-step test, Room, Object, Route and Use, so that you find the problem before you buy instead of on delivery day.

You will draw the room, measure its walls, openings and obstacles, record the piece’s width, depth, height and diagonal, check the route it must travel, and tape its footprint on the floor. It includes three original diagrams, a worked example with made-up numbers and a printable sheet. For walkways once the furniture is in, see [Small Living Room Layout: Keep a 30–36-Inch Walkway](/posts/small-living-room-layout-clear-walkway/); for measuring a table before choosing a game, see [Board Game Night Without a Game Room](/posts/board-game-night-reset-without-a-game-room/). This page covers measuring and fit only.

**Quick answer:** draw the room and measure every wall, door, window and obstacle, then record the piece’s width, depth, height and diagonal. Measure every opening on its route with the door open, tape the footprint on the floor, and live with the tape for a day before you buy.

## 1. Draw the Room Before You Measure It

IKEA’s room-measuring guide starts with paper, not with the tape. You draw a floor plan from above, plus separate front-view drawings of the walls, and you write every number on them. The guide asks for a pencil, a ruler, an eraser and a measuring tape, and it tells you to take all measurements in one unit, which it gives as millimeters or centimeters. In the U.S. inches are fine; just write the unit on the sheet and keep to it.

Start by outlining the room and lettering the walls A, B, C and so on. Mark every door and window on the plan and draw any beams and columns. The guide says to leave current furniture off the drawing, which is the right call because you are measuring the room, not the clutter in it. IKEA recommends a tape of 5 meters or longer, which is about 16 feet, so you can run a whole wall without joining two readings.

![A top-view floor plan with lettered walls, a window, a door, a column and arrows showing the clockwise measuring order](../images/measure-room-floor-plan-clockwise.svg)

## 2. Measure Walls, Openings and Obstacles

Measure each wall from edge to edge and write the figure beside it on the plan. Then start at a corner and work clockwise, measuring from the corner to each window and door. The guide says to include the frame around a window or door in those figures. Measure from the floor to the bottom of each window as well as the overall window height, and write the ceiling height in the middle of the drawing.

Next come the things that block furniture. IKEA’s guide asks for beam drops and columns, with their width and thickness, and for every obstacle on a wall, such as protruding columns, pipes and electrical switchboards. For each obstacle, measure from the nearest wall edge to the center of the point, then record its width and height and the height from the floor to its bottom. Add the items that people forget: baseboards, radiators, vents and outlets. They do not look like obstacles until a bookcase sits two inches too proud of the wall.

A room does not have to be a perfect rectangle for this to work. Letter every wall, measure each one separately and record where each opening sits, rather than assuming that opposite walls are equal.

## 3. Measure the Piece: Width, Depth, Height and Diagonal

Williams-Sonoma’s measuring guide for sofas and sectionals asks you to jot down the width, depth and height of the piece you are considering, including the height of the legs if there are any. It also lists the diagonal depth, and it says the diagonal depth must be less than the width of the opening the piece has to pass through. That is a measurement most buyers never look for, and it often decides whether a sofa can turn through a doorway.

Read a product page carefully. Check whether the listed height includes the legs, whether the width includes the arms, and whether the depth includes a back cushion that can be removed. Write down the numbers for the assembled piece and, if the retailer states them, for each separate module. If the piece has a part that moves, such as a recliner, a drawer or a door, note how far it extends, because section 7 needs that figure.

## 4. Map the Route From the Truck to the Room

Anthropologie’s furniture fit guide recommends a walk-through of the path from the delivery truck to your home, including patios, gardens, sidewalks, apartment lobbies and entrances, and it asks you to measure fixed widths such as porch steps. Inside, measure the height and width of every doorway and hallway on the path, and account for handrails, doorknobs, trim and hinges that cannot be removed. Measure the clearance from the door to the nearest wall or immovable object, because a piece that has to turn needs room to swing.

For an elevator, the guide asks for the size of the door opening and the interior height, width and depth. For stairs, it asks for the width of the stairway, the width and depth of landings, and the clearance from the top and bottom of the stairs to the wall and ceiling. Note anything that hangs in the way, such as light fixtures, wall art, plants, fire sprinklers, handrails and low or angled ceilings, and look for an alternative path if there is one. If necessary, the guide says interior doors can be removed before delivery.

## 5. Measure the Door Opening, Not the Door

The number on a doorframe is not the number a sofa sees. The U.S. Access Board’s standard for door openings, written for accessible routes, measures the clear width of a swinging door between the face of the door and the stop, with the door open 90 degrees. It sets a minimum of 32 inches for that clear width. That is a requirement for accessible routes in public buildings and facilities, not a rule for homes, and your doorway may be narrower or wider. The useful part is the method.

![A top view of a door open to 90 degrees, with the clear opening measured from the door face to the stop and the larger frame opening marked as the wrong figure](../images/measure-doorway-clear-opening.svg)

Open the door to 90 degrees and measure from its face to the stop. Check the narrowest point, because a knob, a latch or a hinge can take more away. Compare that figure with the piece’s diagonal depth, and then ask whether the door can come off its hinges for delivery. If it can, measure the opening again with the door removed.

## 6. Tape the Footprint on the Floor

Both Anthropologie and Williams-Sonoma recommend marking the piece’s outline on the floor with painter’s tape. Anthropologie adds that you should watch access to outlets, light switches, vents and entryways, and compare the size and scale with your current furniture. Williams-Sonoma says to mark the area where you would like to place the piece with painter’s tape, and it offers a room planner for placing furniture to scale.

Leave the tape down for a day. Walk the route you use every morning, open the door that swings nearest, and look at the line from the doorway as a visitor would. The U.S. Access Board’s accessible-route standard is a handy yardstick for a path: it sets 36 inches as the minimum clear width of a walking surface, and it allows 32 inches for a run of no more than 24 inches if wider segments sit on either side of the narrow one. It is not a home standard, but it gives you a public number to compare with your own tape.

## 7. Leave Room to Use the Piece

A footprint tells you where the furniture stands, not what it needs when you use it. Sit where you would sit, stretch your legs, and reach for a cup. Open every door and pull out every drawer that is part of the piece, and extend a recliner or a sofa bed if it has one. Measure the space those motions use and add it to the footprint on your sheet.

There is no single number that suits every room, so record what you needed in yours. If using the piece blocks the walkway you taped in section 6, the answer is a smaller piece or a different position, not a tighter squeeze. ## 8. Worked Example: A Sofa, a Doorway and a Hallway

These numbers are made up to show the method. A living room measures 144 by 126 inches, which is 12 feet by 10 feet 6 inches. A sofa on the product page is 84 inches wide, 38 deep and 34 high, and its listed diagonal depth is 32 inches. The delivery route has a front door, a hallway and one interior door into the living room.

The hallway is 41 inches wide, so the sofa’s 38-inch depth clears it, and so does the diagonal depth. The interior door is the problem. With the door open to 90 degrees, the clear opening between the door face and the stop measures 30 inches, which is less than the 32-inch diagonal depth. With the door lifted off its hinges the opening measures 33½ inches, so the sofa passes with 1½ inches to spare. In the room, the sofa takes 84 of the 144 inches along one wall and 38 of the 126 inches across, leaving 88 inches between it and the opposite wall, so a 36-inch walkway is easy to keep.

| Check | Example result | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Sofa footprint in the room | 84 × 38 in in a 144 × 126 in room | Pass |
| Hallway width against 38 in depth | 41 in | Pass |
| Interior door, door on | 30 in clear opening | Does not pass |
| Interior door, door off | 33½ in clear opening | Pass with 1½ in to spare |
| Walkway in front of the sofa | 88 in to the far wall | Pass |
| Stairs or elevator | Not on this route | Not needed |

The verdict is a conditional yes. The sofa fits if the door can come off its hinges, and the delivery crew knows to do it. If the door cannot come off, the example says re-measure or choose a smaller or modular piece, not guess.

## 9. Pass, Fail or Measure Again

Use three verdicts on every line of your own sheet. Pass means the piece clears the figure with room to spare. Fail means it is larger than the figure, and the fix is a different piece, a different route or a removable door. Measure again means two readings disagree, or the narrowest point was not checked. A line marked measure again is a stop sign, not a pass.

Do not accept a single diagonal reading as proof. Turning, handrails, door swings and the way a crew carries the piece are separate factors, and a tape cannot see them all. That is why the route walk in section 4 comes before the purchase.

## 11. Common Mistakes

The most common error is measuring the room but not the route. The second is reading the frame opening of a door instead of the clear opening with the door open. The third is trusting a product page without checking what the numbers include. Others are mixing units on one sheet, forgetting baseboards and vents, and skipping the tape because the numbers look fine on paper.

If the numbers say yes and the tape says no, believe the tape. It is cheaper to peel up some tape than to pay a restocking fee on a sofa that cannot enter the room.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How do I measure a room for furniture?

Sketch the room from above, letter each wall, and measure every wall from edge to edge. Then measure the door and window positions from each corner, the ceiling height, and any beams, columns, vents or outlets. Leave existing furniture off the drawing, use one unit throughout, and finish by taping the new piece’s footprint on the floor.


### What measurements do I need before buying a sofa?

You need the sofa’s width, depth and height, including legs if it has them, plus its diagonal depth. Compare those with the room, with every doorway, hallway and stairway on the route, and with the space you need to use it. Retailers publish the sofa’s dimensions on the product page, so read which parts the numbers include.


### How do I measure a doorway for furniture delivery?

Open the door to 90 degrees and measure the clear opening from the face of the door to the stop, then check the narrowest point, where a knob, hinge or trim may reduce it. Compare that figure with the piece’s diagonal depth, and ask whether the door can come off its hinges for delivery.


### Will my sofa fit through a door or hallway?

Only the measurements can tell you. Compare the sofa’s width, height and diagonal depth with the clear opening of every doorway, hallway and stairway between the truck and the room. A piece that clears a doorway on paper can still jam on a turn, a handrail or a low light fixture, so the walk-through matters as much as the numbers.


### Should I measure in inches or centimeters?

Use whichever your furniture listing uses, and write the unit on every sheet. In the U.S. that is usually inches. The important rule is to avoid mixing units on one drawing, because a figure copied in centimeters and read as inches is a common way to get a plan wrong.


### How do I measure an irregular room?

Draw it as it is rather than forcing a rectangle. Letter every wall, measure each one edge to edge, record each door, window, beam and column from the nearest corner, and add the ceiling height. If a corner does not look square, treat the measurements for each wall as separate facts and tape the footprint on the floor to test the real fit.
